City Games

Playable Systems Design

What

An elective that teaches systems thinking, game design, and AI-augmented design workflows at the same time. Each student picks a site in Boston or Cambridge and builds a playable model of what surrounds it: the people, money, rules, culture, and climate. Then they start a design studio, with a catch: their studio's employees won't be human.

How

Over the semester, students learn systems design at two levels: academically, as a way to interpret their site, and practically, as a way to lead their studio. AI teams are fallible in ways both similar to and different from human teams, so students have to design ways of working together that are robust and critical. If they do, they'll make work faster and stronger than they could alone.

Why

Agents can execute ideas; the ideas, and the learning, have to come from the students. On purpose, and a little ironically, every reflection assignment in this class is handwritten on paper. This class explores systems in a concrete way through its content (a site, a game) and in a theoretical way through its process (leading AI teams). By integrating the two, students will gain a far deeper understanding of systems thinking, and a relevant, instantly applicable skillset for their design practice.

Background on the class

I teach a workshop on collaborating with Claude on design work. In one session, students who have never used AI this way build and publish a small working tool. What one session can't cover is running several agents at once: scoping the project, splitting it into parallel pieces, tracking what each agent did, checking the work, and improving the process as you go.

Architects running offices and producers running crews have faced these problems for a long time, and most of the readings were written about teams of people. My hypothesis is that much of what they found carries over to teams that include agents, and some of it doesn't. The course tests that.

I've also spent the past year designing an online multiplayer board game, Pastry Pirates, by directing agents to build every part of it, including the systems we used to work together. That process, more than the game, is what this course teaches: how to lead, how to design critically, and how to communicate and collaborate effectively with both human and non-human coworkers.

Claude vs Other

The course uses Claude, Claude Code, Git, and GitHub because they're what I use every day, and they get students with no coding experience to working software fast. Grading is based on students' decisions, which carry over to any tool. Every week includes time to look for what the agents got wrong.

How the work is graded

Grading follows the decisions students make: what they chose to model, what they asked agents to build, what they kept, what they overruled, and why.

Weekly work · 30%

Assignments, handwritten reflections, rule changes, simulations, and playtest notes.

Mid review · 20%

A playable game, a 60-second pitch, and a response to feedback: the top 3 pieces taken on, 3 others rejected with reasons.

Final game · 35%

The published game, played at Game Con on launch day, and a handwritten final reflection.

Devlog · 15%

One 60-second video in week 12 on what failed and what changed because of it.

Reflections and the decision tracker

Reflections are handwritten. Each names a decision the student made, one an agent made that they kept, and one they overruled.

I'll also pilot a decision tracker that logs which choices came from the student and which from the agents. It's ungraded in its first year, since a log can't yet tell a decision from an edit. Its job is to help students write an honest reflection.

What already exists, and the gap

Harvard's CS2680 and Stanford's CS146S teach engineers to run coding agents, with no design content. Most design-school courses teach how generative models work (SCI 6485, Columbia's Spatial AI) or ask what AI means for the discipline (MIT 4.184, Illinois ARCH 594).

At the GSD, Eric Plosky's SCI-6515 covers who decides how data and AI get used in planning, and Alexandros Haridis's SCI-6508 covers the theory of computation in design. Neither teaches designers to direct agents. Daniel D'Oca's studios use games as a design medium; this course would add the modeling underneath. I'd love to hear what all three think.

Placement is my own reading of each course description, not a measurement.

If this interests you

This is a course idea. I'm sharing it to see whether anyone at the GSD wants it taught, in this form or a smaller one. To make it real, it would need:

  1. A faculty champion. Or a smaller start, as a J-term workshop or a module inside an existing course.

  2. Tool access. A way into Harvard's Claude license through HUIT, or students paying about $100 for the term.

  3. Room in the curriculum alongside SCI-6515, SCI-6508, and anything I've missed.

  4. A conversation with Daniel D'Oca, so this builds on his studios' planning games rather than duplicating them.

  5. A small cohort. About 16 students, with a few seats for MIT and other Harvard schools, so the whole class can play every game.
