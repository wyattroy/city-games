City Games

Playable Systems Design

An elective that teaches systems thinking, game design and AI-augmented design workflows at the same time. Each student picks a site in Boston or Cambridge and builds a playable model of what surrounds it: the people, money, rules, culture and climate. Then they start a design studio, with a catch: their studio's employees won't be human.

Over the semester, students learn systems design at two levels: academically, as a way to interpret their site, and practically, as a way to lead their studio. AI teams are fallible in ways both similar to and different from human teams, so students have to design ways of working together that are robust and critical. If they do, they'll make work faster and stronger than they could alone.

On purpose, and a little ironically, every reflection assignment in this class is handwritten on paper. Agents can execute ideas; the ideas, and the learning, have to come from the students.

Where this comes from

I've been teaching a workshop on collaborating with Claude to execute design work. In one session, students who have never used AI this way design, iterate on and publish a small working tool. What a single session can't cover is everything that starts once you're running several agents at once: constraining the project, directing how it starts, splitting it so each piece can run in parallel, keeping track of what each agent did, checking the work, and critically iterating on both the work and your own process.

Those are old questions. Architects running offices, film producers running crews and planners running public processes have been answering them for a long time, and most of the readings here were written about teams of people. My hypothesis is that a good deal of what they found carries over to teams that include agents, and that some of it doesn't. Testing that, reading by reading, is the spine of the course.

I've also spent the past year designing an online multiplayer board game, Pastry Pirates, by directing agents to build every part of it, including the systems we used to work together. That process, more than the game, is what this course teaches: how to lead, how to design critically, and how to communicate and collaborate effectively with both human and non-human coworkers.

What I want to name up front

The course is built mostly on one company's tools, Claude and Claude Code, plus Git and GitHub. I chose them because they're what I use every day and because they let students with no coding experience get to working software fast. Grading is based on the decisions students make, and those carry over to any tool.

The stance is a critical designer's. Students build a lot, and every week includes time to look for what the agents got wrong, what they made easier to skip, and whose judgment ended up mattering.

The city as a designed system

Each student picks one real site in Boston or Cambridge and spends the semester modeling its context as a game that strangers can play: who lives there, who pays for it, what the zoning allows, what the river will do in thirty years, what used to stand there. Then they find out where the model is wrong.

Games already have a foothold at the GSD. Daniel D'Oca's studios have assigned tabletop planning games since 2011, Rem Koolhaas and Stephan Petermann's spring 2026 studio produced games about ancient Rome, and Harvard Design Magazine argued in July 2026 that games are a form of design research. The lineage runs back further: Lizzie Magie's Landlord's Game (1904) was an argument about land, and SimCity drew on Jay Forrester's Urban Dynamics. This course adds the discipline of modeling underneath: balance, simulation, playtesting with strangers, and rulebooks precise enough to be wrong, with agents doing the heavy lifting of building and running the simulators.

What it costs

Harvard's Claude licenses, which include Claude Code, currently cover students in the College, GSAS, SEAS, GSE, HKS and SPH. GSD students aren't on that list, and neither are cross-registered MIT students. GSD policy doesn't let course budgets pay for students' AI tools, and asks that the expected cost appear in the syllabus, the same way fabrication materials do.

Every assignment is scoped to fit within Claude Pro, which includes Claude Code. The higher Max tier is never required. If HUIT could add students in this course to Harvard's license, the cost would drop to zero.

One site, one game, all semester

Every student designs one game about one site from week 1 to week 13. Every game is digital, built by a team of agents the student directs, from a first playable browser prototype in week 3 to the published version on launch day. It ends as something a stranger can play from a link, with a rulebook, the simulator that tuned it, documentation of the playtests, and a handwritten postmortem.

What changes at each stage of a project

Agents can do much of the execution in design. When generating fifty options takes minutes, the work shifts to framing the question and judging the results, which is close to what the head of a practice does all day: briefing, delegating and reviewing. How close is one of the questions the course tests. The matrix shows which weeks practice each stage.

Thirteen weeks, one game

Each week moves the same game forward one design step, and pairs that step with one way of running a team of agents. The readings are potential ones: a starting list I'd narrow to one or two a week before teaching.

How the work is graded

Grading follows the decisions students make: what they chose to model, what they asked agents to build, what they kept, what they overruled, and why. In week 3 the class co-writes the rubric. I bring the five criteria below, and each student adds criteria that apply only to their own game, since a game about zoning and a game about flooding shouldn't be measured identically.

A 60-second video every other week on what failed and what changed because of it.

Decision memos, the tracker and the devlog

Each milestone comes with a short handwritten memo: the most important decisions the student made, one the agents made that they kept, one they overruled, and the evidence behind each. The memos and the crits are what get graded.

Alongside the memos I'll pilot a decision tracker, a tool I'm building that logs which choices came from the student and which from the agents. It's ungraded in its first year. A log can't yet reliably tell a decision from an edit, so its job is to help students write an honest memo, not to grade them.

Every other week students also record a 60-second devlog about the most important thing that failed and what they changed because of it. It's quicker to watch than a log is to read, and it's graded on how specific it is, since a failure can be performed as easily as it can be written.

What already exists, and the gap

I looked at current and recent courses at Harvard, MIT, Stanford, Columbia, Carnegie Mellon and Illinois. Harvard's CS2680 and Stanford's CS146S teach engineers to run coding agents, and neither has design content. Most design-school courses either teach how generative models work (SCI 6485, Columbia's Spatial AI) or ask what AI means for the discipline (MIT 4.184, Illinois ARCH 594).

The closest courses at the GSD are Eric Plosky's SCI-6515, on the institutions that decide how data and AI get used in planning, and Alexandros Haridis's SCI-6508, on the theory of computation in design. Neither teaches designers to direct agents as a working practice. Daniel D'Oca's studios are the closest on games; they use games as a design medium, and this course would add the modeling discipline underneath. I'd love to hear what all three think of it.

Placement is my own reading of each course description, not a measurement.

If this interests you

This is a course idea, not a submission. I'm sharing it to find out whether anyone at the GSD wants to see it taught, in this form or a smaller one. To make it real, it would need a few things:

  1. A faculty champion. Someone at the GSD who wants to see this taught. It could also start smaller, as a J-term workshop or a module inside an existing course.

  2. Tool access. Either a way into Harvard's Claude license through HUIT, or students paying about $100 for the term.

  3. Room in the curriculum. A sense of how it would sit alongside SCI-6515, SCI-6508 and anything else I've missed.

  4. A conversation with Daniel D'Oca. His studios have used planning games for over a decade, and I'd want this course to build on that rather than duplicate it.

  5. A small cohort. About 16 students, with a few seats for MIT and other Harvard schools. Every game gets played by the whole class, so the size matters.
